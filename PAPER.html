Working paper · Financial engineering

No Free Alpha Benchmarking autonomous LLM trading agents against passive indexing

Across model families, hand-tuned strategy playbooks, and a regime-switching meta-agent (2020–2025), no autonomous LLM trading agent produced a dependable edge over passive indexing. Run-to-run model decision noise — 30–60 return-points on the identical configuration — dominates any edge, most apparent “beating SPY” is small-cap beta, and the one durable, useful property is a single agent’s bear-market capital preservation, now being validated live.

Revised 2026-11-23 (first draft 2026-10-29) · AI-assisted, human-directed (see Appendix C)

Abstract

We test whether autonomous large-language-model (LLM) trading agents can beat a passive benchmark in US equities, and — when one appears to — whether the edge is real, reproducible, and improvable. Agents built on Google Gemini, OpenAI, and Anthropic models trade a rules-enforced, point-in-time simulator (long-only $3–$50 universe, T+1 execution, mandatory stops) against a passive SPY benchmark, a small-cap IWM benchmark, and a random-trading control. In the primary study (Gemini, 2024–2025, eight quarters) no persona beat SPY on a risk-adjusted basis and a random control matched or beat all of them. Extending the study exposes a deeper result: (i) the single standout, Anthropic Claude Haiku 5.5 (aggressive), decayed from a 71% to a pooled 60% beat-rate over repeated runs; (ii) apparent SPY-beating was largely small-cap beta that reversed against IWM and in a large-cap-led year (2021); (iii) two hand-tuned playbook variants were statistically indistinguishable from the baseline, with the ranking inverting between runs of the same year; and (iv) a 200-day-MA regime-switching meta-agent whipsawed and underperformed both of its components. The common cause is LLM run-to-run decision variance that exceeds any edge — the only stable lines in the study are the ones with no model in them. The lone durable, useful property is the baseline agent’s bear-market capital preservation (near-flat across ~5 runs of 2022 while SPY fell 22%, by going to cash), now under live paper-trading validation. We release the full benchmark, tests, and reproduction workflows. The result is a clean negative on “LLMs as a dependable trading strategy,” with one narrow, real capital-preservation property worth watching.

1Introduction

LLMs can now call tools, read market data, and emit structured decisions, prompting a natural question: can an LLM run a portfolio and beat the market? Public demonstrations tend to report a single impressive run, which is exactly the setup most vulnerable to look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, and small-sample luck.

This project asks the question under conditions designed to make a positive result hard to fake: a deterministic simulator that enforces trading rules in code; strict point-in-time data so a historical agent cannot see the future; passive SPY and small-cap IWM benchmarks; a random-trading control to calibrate luck; and a paired bootstrap over pooled quarters so the verdict carries statistical weight. When a candidate edge did appear, we then did what single-run demonstrations never do: we tried to break it — repeating runs, testing other model families, correcting the benchmark, hand-tuning the strategy, and building a regime-switcher to combine the best behaviors.

Research question. Does an autonomous LLM agent beat passive indexing on a risk-adjusted basis; if so, is the edge reproducible; and can it be engineered into something dependable?

Contribution. (1) An open, rules-enforced, look-ahead-free benchmark with passive (SPY), like-for-like (IWM), and random controls. (2) A statistical protocol — daily-return risk metrics plus a pooled paired bootstrap — that reports the probability an agent beats the market rather than a single return. (3) A clean negative result that survives three independent attempts to engineer an edge (more capable models, hand-tuned playbooks, regime-switching), all defeated by the same cause: model decision noise larger than any signal. (4) One isolated, durable, income-relevant property (bear-market capital preservation) and a live forward test of it.

Headline finding. No LLM agent produced a dependable edge. The identical configuration swings 30–60 return-points between runs; the only stable performers are the deterministic benchmark lines. Most “wins” over SPY were small-cap beta. The one real, reproducible property is defensive: the baseline agent preserves capital in bear markets by moving to cash.

2Background & related work

LLM agents in finance. A growing body of demonstrations wires LLMs to market data and tools and reports trading performance. Most share two weaknesses: they evaluate over short windows, and they do not rigorously prevent the model from accessing information unavailable at the simulated decision time. A third, which this study foregrounds, is nondeterminism: a single reported run says little when the same agent’s output varies widely between runs.

Backtesting pitfalls. The standard hazards are well known: look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, data-snooping, and ignoring costs. An LLM adds a subtler channel: a model trained through some cutoff may “remember” how a past period resolved, so a backtest must also deny it live tools that would reveal the future. And because LLM output is stochastic, a backtest must be repeated, not run once.

Efficient markets as the null. The efficient-market hypothesis predicts that, net of risk and costs, active strategies should not reliably beat a passive benchmark. The interesting outcome is not “the agent made money” (a rising market does that) but “the agent beat buy-and-hold on a risk-adjusted basis, repeatably.” This motivates our reference points — passive SPY, like-for-like IWM, and a random control — and our focus on pooled, risk-adjusted statistics over single-run returns.

3Experimental design

3.1 Simulation environment (“Code is Law”)

3.2 Agents and personas

Each agent is a model × persona; personas (conservative / balanced / aggressive) differ only in a system-prompt risk mandate. The primary study uses Gemini 3.8 Flash (temperature 0); it was later extended to OpenAI gpt-6-luna and Anthropic Claude Haiku 5.5 on the balanced and aggressive personas. The tuning arc (§4.5–4.7) adds three Claude-Haiku variants sharing the same model: robust (ATR-based stops, volatility-targeted sizing, earlier de-risk), return (trailing-only profit-taking, a re-entry rule), and switch (a meta-agent that each day picks the robust or baseline playbook by SPY’s 200-day MA). Because a standing playbook is just the agent’s system prompt, a “variant” is the same model reading different instructions. A random control (no LLM) trades randomly within the same rules and daily watchlist — the skill-versus-luck baseline.

3.3 Data

Prices from Yahoo Finance (yfinance); macro indicators from FRED. Both free. Historical prices are cached on disk and retried with backoff; non-finite marks are rejected and carried forward so a single bad price cannot corrupt an account.

3.4 Point-in-time integrity (no look-ahead)

A backtest for a past date must not see the future. Enforced: (a) prices come from a window ending at the simulation date; (b) FRED series are fetched as-of that date; (c) date-aware tools receive the as-of date, while tools that can only return the present (web search, news, fundamentals) are disabled on a past date; (d) the live market narrative and catalyst watchlist are suppressed for historical snapshots; (e) the regime signal (§4.7) uses only closes up to the decision date. The honest cost: historical agents trade on a leaner information set than a live agent would — the correct trade-off, since fabricated as-of news would be worse.

3.5 Benchmarks

Three reference points over the identical window: SPY buy-and-hold (passive, large-cap), IWM buy-and-hold (Russell 2000, small-cap — the like-for-like benchmark, since the agents trade a $3–$50 small-cap universe and SPY flatters them), and the random control (luck baseline).

3.6 Statistical method

Per run we report return, maximum drawdown, annualized volatility, and Sharpe (rf = 0). To answer “beats SPY?” we use a paired bootstrap: the same resampled days are applied to agent and SPY together, terminal wealth compared, and we report the probability the agent beats SPY and the margin distribution. Per-day pairs are pooled across quarters before bootstrapping. Critically, because LLM calls are stochastic even at temperature 0, we repeat each configuration and read conclusions from the spread across runs, not any single path. The simulator and analysis scripts are covered by stdlib-only unit tests in CI.

4Results

4.1 Primary study (Gemini, 2024–2025): no persona beat SPY

All eight quarters ran cleanly. Two-year compounded ($10,000 start):

Pooled paired bootstrap (~500 day-pairs, 1-yr horizon; near 50% = no edge): random 59%, balanced 40%, aggressive 27%, conservative 12%. No agent reaches a coin-flip; the dart-throwing control beats all three. A separate 2026 Q2 run reproduced the ordering (no persona beat SPY on return or Sharpe). §5 explains why the random control “won” (lottery-ticket concentration + buy-and-hold beta), and why it is still not skill.

4.2 Cross-model extension: one standout, then it decays

Adding OpenAI and Anthropic (balanced + aggressive, same eight quarters): both OpenAI personas trailed SPY (gpt-6-luna balanced 25% / aggressive 13% beat-rate), and Claude’s balanced trailed (13%). The one exception, Claude Haiku 5.5 aggressive, looked spectacular on its first run — +84% over two years, a 71% beat-rate. But repeating the identical run three times showed the edge decaying every time — 71% → 61% → 45% beat-rate; +84% → +62% → +44% compounded, the third run landing below SPY. Pooled, the honest estimate is a modest 60% beat-rate / +4.4% median margin, not the market-beater it first appeared. (A notable null: handing this exact playbook to gpt-6-luna did not reproduce the behavior — it lost −24% in a 2022 bear — so the property is model-specific, not prompt-portable.)

4.3 The one durable property: bear-market capital preservation

Where the bull-market edge is noisy, the baseline claude_aggressive in a bear market is the project’s most reproducible good result. Across runs of 2022 (SPY −21.8%) it ended near flat — −0.0 / −4.3 / −2.4 / +0.0 / +0.8%, with one −10.0% bad-draw tail — beating SPY by ~20 points each time, with single-digit drawdowns, by going to cash (up to 100% cash, sitting out the worst quarters). This is capital preservation, not profit, but for a drawdown-averse objective it is exactly the right shape, and it does not decay on repetition the way the bull edge does.

4.4 Benchmark correction: “beating SPY” was mostly small-cap beta

The agents trade a $3–$50 small-cap universe; SPY is large-cap. Against the like-for-like IWM benchmark the apparent edge largely vanishes, and in 2021 — a large-cap-led bull — every agent and IWM itself trailed SPY badly:

So the agents were never beating “the market”; they were long small-caps, which beat SPY in 2020 (crash-recovery) and 2022 (bear) and lost to it in 2021. The 2020/2022 “wins” were the universe, not skill.

4.5 Tuning adds no edge — only reshuffled noise

Two hand-tuned playbook variants (robust, return) were run head-to-head with the baseline on identical data. On a cherry-picked 2-quarter probe robust looked spectacular; on a full held-out year it did not reproduce, and repeated full-year runs inverted the ranking:

The baseline went from best to worst between two runs of the same year. The between-agent gaps (~10–20 pts) are smaller than the within-agent run-to-run spread (~40–60 pts): tuning the playbook does not add a detectable edge.

4.7 Regime-switching made it worse

Since robust won bulls and the baseline won bears, we built claude_switch: each day, point-in-time, it runs the robust playbook when SPY is above its 200-day MA (risk-on) and the cash-defensive baseline when below (risk-off). The mechanism worked — risk-on through 2021, flipping risk-off when SPY broke its 200-DMA on 2022-01-21 — but then whipsawed seven times in two months as 2022 oscillated around the average, and ended the bear below both of its components:

A switch that lands below both parts is the signature of the switching itself destroying value. Naïve 200-DMA regime-timing added a new failure mode (whipsaw and lag) without a reliable benefit.

4.8 Live forward test (in progress)

Backtests carry upward biases (survivorship, hindsight-built watchlist, idealized fills). A daily paper-trade harness trades the baseline claude_aggressive — the agent the evidence favors — plus a random control, on live, unseen days with fake money (from Nov 2026). Early days confirm the mechanics and, encouragingly, the same defensive character seen in backtests: it holds one small position at ~90% cash rather than churning, and on a down day fell far less than the market. It is too early for a performance read; the real test is a live drawdown. This is the only part of the study still accumulating, and it is the honest arbiter of everything above.

5Case analysis: why the random control “won”

The control’s high returns are not evidence that randomness trades well; they show how misleading raw return is without a risk and luck lens. Lottery-ticket concentration: its best quarters were carried by one or two high-beta small-caps that happened to multiply. Buy-and-hold beta vs agent churn: all agents draw from the same volatile $3–$50 watchlist; the random agent mostly buys and holds, and in windows where those small-caps ran, passively holding high-beta names beat both SPY and the LLM agents, who traded the same names actively — selling winners early and getting stopped out on noise. Why it is still not skill: the control is the riskiest participant (high volatility, deep drawdowns, and in 2022 it lost ~50%). Its high-variance mean swung from +140% (2020) to −53% (2022). The lesson is not “random is good” but “the window favored high-beta buy-and-hold, and the agents were beaten by a dart-thrower” — the sharpest evidence of no alpha.

6Discussion

Decision noise dominates. The central empirical fact is that the same configuration swings 30–60 return-points between runs. This is larger than any edge we could find or engineer, and it is why every improvement attempt failed: more capable models (§4.2), hand-tuned playbooks (§4.5), and regime-switching (§4.7) each tried to tune a signal smaller than the noise. The only stable performers in the entire study are the deterministic benchmark lines.

Beta, not alpha. Correcting the benchmark (§4.4) dissolves most of the apparent skill: the agents were long small-caps, and “beating SPY” tracked when small-caps beat large-caps, reversing in 2021. Against the like-for-like IWM the edge is negligible.

Active churn is a tax in a trending market. As in the primary study, agents repeatedly traded out of names that then ran, paying slippage and stop-outs. LLMs reproduce this familiar failure rather than escape it.

The one real property is defensive. The exception to all of the above is the baseline’s bear-market cash-defense (§4.3) — reproducible, model-specific, and income-relevant in shape. It is not alpha (it preserves, it does not generate), but it is the one behavior that survived every robustness check, which is why it is the one we are testing live.

On single runs. A single Claude-aggressive run looked like a market-beater (+84%, 71%); a single robust probe looked spectacular (+30.9%). Both dissolved on repetition. Any claim built on one or two runs — the usual format for “my AI beats the market” demonstrations — would here have been noise.

7Limitations

These bound the claim to “in this setup, these agents did not beat the market dependably.” They do not establish that LLMs cannot trade.

  • Run-to-run noise is the dominant limitation. Single-run numbers (including the single-run OpenAI/Gemini results) are unreliable; conclusions rest on repeated runs and on the deterministic benchmark lines.
  • Regimes span 2020–2025 (crash-recovery, bull, bear) but each regime-and-agent cell is only 1–3 runs — enough to show noise dominates, not to pin small effects.
  • The durable positive is one model, with a bad-draw tail, and awaits live confirmation (§4.8).
  • Ensembling is untested. Averaging many runs per day would shrink the noise ~1/√N; cost precluded testing it, so “tuning cannot fix the noise” is shown for the model/playbook/regime-switch levers but not for ensembling.
  • Lean historical information set (no as-of news), free-data caveats (yfinance survivorship and adjusted-price quirks; a watchlist of current small-caps), FRED vintage (observation-date filtering, not ALFRED), and no explicit transaction costs beyond slippage.

8Conclusion and future work

Within a rigorous benchmark — rules enforced in code, strictly point-in-time data, passive and like-for-like benchmarks, a random control, and repeated runs — no LLM agent produced a dependable edge over passive indexing, and the three main ways to engineer one (more capable models, hand-tuned playbooks, regime-switching) all failed to the same cause: the model’s run-to-run decision variance is larger than any edge. Most apparent SPY-beating was small-cap beta that reversed against the right benchmark. The one durable, useful property is the baseline agent’s bear-market capital preservation, now under live validation. This is a clean negative result on “LLMs as a dependable trading strategy,” with one narrow, real capital-preservation property worth watching — arguably more useful than a noisy positive.

The decisive open question is variance, not strategy. The untested lever is ensembling — averaging many runs per day to shrink decision noise ~1/√N — which is the only avenue not closed here and the natural next study, alongside point-in-time vendor data, explicit transaction costs, and a longer live forward test. The code, tests, and one-click reproduction workflows are released so these extensions can be run directly.

Appendix

A. Reproducibility

The benchmark runs via GitHub Actions. Backtest (date range) runs a single window; Batch Backtest (multi-quarter) runs quarters as a matrix and pools them into a combined verdict. Inputs: agents, max_tool_calls, quarters, and strict_prices (strict price-integrity on for a final audited run, off for iterative exploration). A live paper-trade workflow runs the baseline daily and commits a ledger. The simulator and analysis scripts are covered by stdlib-only unit tests in CI.

B. Enforced constraints

Long-only; $3–$50 price band at fill; T+1 at the next open with 0.1% slippage; stops/targets vs daily High/Low with gap-throughs filling at the open; 5–25% per position; ≤15 positions; ≤3 new buys/day; mandatory stop-loss on every buy; one frozen daily OHLC set shared across agents; non-finite prices rejected and carried forward. Starting capital $10,000 per agent per run.
